# You're Not Behind (Yet): How to Learn AI in 19 Minutes

## Overview

This AI Teaching outlines Ali Abdaal's 5-phase process for becoming fluent and productive with AI tools, as presented in the transcript. The method progresses from building foundational habits in week one, to using AI as a thinking coach in week two, delegating tasks to AI in weeks three and four, systematizing AI usage with a prompt library in months one to two, and eventually automating workflows from month four onwards. Fluency, as defined in the transcript, means being able to use AI as a default tool for thinking, creating, and executing work—not just as a search replacement. It is designed for anyone who wants to move from AI beginner to AI-fluent by following a structured, habit-based approach.

## When to Follow These AI Teachings

- When you want to build consistent AI usage habits from scratch in a structured timeline
- When you want to move beyond using AI as a simple search replacement and use it for coaching, delegation, and automation
- When you need a clear week-by-week or month-by-month roadmap for increasing AI fluency
- When you want to build a reusable prompt library and automate repetitive tasks in your job or business

## Steps

### Step 1: Build Your Foundations (Week 1)

Set up five non-negotiable foundations before attempting advanced AI workflows.

1. Use AI for the same types of questions you would normally search Google for: definitions, how-to instructions, quick fact checks, research starting points, and everyday problem-solving. Pick one primary AI tool—Claude, ChatGPT, Grok, or Gemini—and use it exclusively for all of these query types during week one.
2. Keep your chosen AI website open in a pinned tab at all times while working. The goal is to have an AI chat window always available on screen.
3. Use voice input instead of keyboard typing. Use built-in dictation on Windows and Mac, built-in AI dictation inside the AI tools, or tools like WhisperFlow. Speaking lets you input ideas faster and more naturally.
4. Download the mobile apps for your chosen AI tools. This lets you use AI as a Google replacement or thinking buddy anywhere, including while walking, commuting, or traveling.
5. Automatically record and transcribe your online meetings and ideally your in-person meetings too. Use free tools such as Fathom for automatic transcription, or tools like Grain for meeting recording and transcription.

### Step 2: Use AI as Your Coach (Week 2)

Start using AI to help you think better about the work you are already doing. Do not yet ask AI to do your work for you.

1. Ask role-specific coaching questions. For example, if you are a social media manager, ask: "I am responsible for growing an Instagram profile from 1 million to 1.2 million followers in 90 days. What are the highest leverage actions I should focus on? What mistakes do people in my role commonly make?"
2. Ask AI to interview you about your job. Use a prompt such as: "I want you to interview me about what I actually do in my role and help me identify what is high-leverage and what is probably a waste of time."
3. Feed meeting transcripts into AI for analysis. For example: "This is a transcript of a conversation with my manager coaching me on Instagram strategy. Based on this conversation, suggest a curriculum for me to follow over the next 2 weeks to improve my skills."
4. Ask AI to analyze coaching sessions or teaching sessions to extract key themes and student struggles, and to give feedback on your teaching style and blind spots.
5. Treat AI as a smart colleague who reads a lot but lacks context on your specific situation. Review its advice critically rather than blindly following it.

### Step 3: Use AI as Your Worker (Weeks 3-4)

Begin asking AI to complete the middle 80% of tasks while you handle the first 10% (initial setup and direction) and the final 10% (quality review and final adjustments), following the 10/80/10 rule (attributed to Dan Martell's book *Buy Back Your Time*).

1. Apply the 10/80/10 rule: you complete the first 10% of the task, you ask AI to complete the middle 80%, and you complete the final 10% for quality review and final adjustments.
2. Provide rich context before asking AI to generate outputs. For example, instead of saying "come up with 50 Instagram content ideas," provide a recent transcript, competitor examples, and your current content strategy doc, then ask for 20 hook ideas focused on counterintuitive takes and pattern interrupts.
3. Curate the AI output by selecting the five best ideas, then paste those five back into the AI and ask it to generate additional ideas using the same constraints (e.g., counterintuitive angles, pattern interrupts, under-20-word hooks). This iterative refinement expands your options without sacrificing quality.
4. Treat weak AI output as feedback that your prompt needs refinement, not as a reason to abandon AI. Refine prompts by adding constraints such as "avoid rhetorical questions" or "keep each hook under 20 words."
5. Experiment broadly by asking yourself whenever you are doing any task: "Can the AI handle part of this task?"

### Step 4: Use AI as a System (Months 1-2)

Convert your best prompts into a reusable prompt library so your AI performance improves over time.

1. Start versioning your prompts using a numbered format: v1 is your first draft, v2 adds at least one additional constraint (e.g., "avoid rhetorical questions," "keep each hook under 20 words") or additional context, and each subsequent version tracks what changed and why. Store versions in a dedicated document or note-taking app.
2. Build prompts for each recurring workflow you have. For example, a social media manager should have prompts for hook generation, turning transcripts into LinkedIn posts, and analyzing competitor accounts.
3. Use a text-expansion tool such as Text Expander (macOS/Windows) to assign keyboard shortcuts to your most-used prompts. For example, typing "IGH" expands into the full Instagram hook-generation prompt.
4. Test each prompt in your library against at least two AI models (e.g., GPT-4o, Claude Sonnet, Gemini Pro) and record which model produces the best output for each prompt type. If a paid tier consistently outperforms the free tier for your most-used prompts, subscribe to that tier.
5. Identify use-case-specific tools that replace or augment chat-only workflows for tasks in your domain, such as slide-deck creation (Gamma, Beautiful.ai, Figma Slides). Add these to your toolkit only after you have a clear recurring need for them.
6. Limit your active toolset to 3–5 tools that directly serve your primary work tasks (e.g., one primary chat AI, one text-expansion tool, one automation platform). Adding more tools before you have mastered these leads to tool overwhelm without proportional benefit.

### Step 5: Use AI as Infrastructure (Month 4 Onwards)

Automate workflows so AI runs in the background without requiring manual chat interaction each time.

1. Level 1 automation: Use AI automation built into tools you already use. For example, use AI features inside Premiere Pro, Descript, or similar media tools to automatically transcribe and route outputs.
2. Level 2 automation: Use connector tools such as Zapier or Make.com to link apps together. For example, automatically take a new Zoom recording, transcribe it, run it through a saved prompt in ChatGPT, and send the output to Slack.
3. Level 3 automation: Use more powerful workflow automation tools such as n8n to build more sophisticated automations with greater control over logic, conditions, and multi-step processes.
4. Level 4 automation: Build your own internal AI tools or apps. Building custom internal AI tools is overkill for most individual knowledge workers and use cases (most needs can be met with simpler tools like Zapier).
5. Before automating anything, decide whether it is actually worth automating, whether it is better done manually, or whether the process itself should be removed entirely.

## Examples

### Example 1: Social Media Manager Using AI as Coach and Worker

Nicole, a social media manager, uses the 5-phase method as follows. In week one, she sets up Claude as her default AI, pins the tab, uses voice input on her phone, and installs the mobile app. In week two, she asks Claude: "I am responsible for growing an Instagram profile from 1 million to 1.2 million followers in 90 days. What are the highest leverage actions I should focus on?" In weeks three and four, she provides a recent video transcript, competitor Reel examples, and the content strategy doc, then asks for 20 hook ideas focused on counterintuitive takes. She selects the best five, feeds them back in, and gets 50 more ideas. By month three, she has a versioned prompt library with keyboard shortcuts, and by month four she automates transcript routing from video editors into her hook-generation workflow.

### Example 2: Student Success Lead Automating Weekly Reporting

Geo, who runs student success for an online mentorship program, applies the 5-phase method to coaching call reporting. In week two, she feeds transcribed coaching session recordings into AI to extract key student struggles and improve the curriculum. By month four, she builds an automation that takes all weekly student coaching call transcripts, combines them with Slack support channel conversations and CRM data from Notion, and generates a weekly report per student summarizing their wins, struggles, and areas needing support. This replaces a manual Friday admin process that takes 3 hours every week.

### Example 3: Business Owner Using AI for Annual Planning

A business owner with a revenue goal of growing from $5 million to $10 million uses the method as follows. In week two, they ask AI to interview them about their annual and quarterly planning, asking questions to uncover the key levers for growth tied to a new product launch. In week three, they use the 10/80/10 rule to draft parts of the annual plan, letting AI structure the content while they refine strategy and tone. By month two, they version the annual planning prompt and add brand context to improve future outputs.

## Best Practices

- ✅ Use AI as a default starting point for questions you would normally Google
- ✅ Keep an AI chat window pinned and visible while working to build the habit of constant access
- ✅ Use voice input to increase your speed and natural language when talking to AI
- ✅ Install mobile AI apps so you can use AI anywhere during your day
- ✅ Automatically record and transcribe meetings so you can feed transcripts back into AI later
- ✅ Treat AI as a thought partner and coach before treating it as a worker
- ✅ Apply the 10/80/10 rule when delegating tasks to AI
- ✅ Provide rich context and examples to the AI before asking for outputs
- ✅ Curate AI outputs with taste and discernment rather than accepting them blindly
- ✅ Iterate on prompts over time, versioning them like a recipe
- ✅ Build a prompt library with keyboard shortcuts for your recurring workflows
- ✅ Test at least three AI models with your prompts and pay for the ones that give better results
- ✅ Start with simple automation tools such as Zapier or Make.com before moving to advanced platforms
- ✅ Before automating a task, ask whether it should be automated, done manually, or removed entirely

## Keep In Mind

- The goal is to develop the habit of turning to AI whenever you are stuck, need to draft content, want to analyze data or transcripts, or want to speed up research and planning tasks in your work or studies
- Taste and discernment are the skills that separate people who use AI well from people who do not: specifically, the ability to evaluate whether an output is good or bad, identify what needs to change, and refine prompts accordingly rather than accepting the first result
- AI is like a very smart colleague who reads a lot of books but who doesn't have much context on anything other than the knowledge that they've gotten from books; its advice should be evaluated, not followed blindly
- The first time you use a prompt, treat it as version one and improve it with each use
- Automating a bad process still results in a bad automated process; improve the process before automating it
- You do not need to use advanced AI automation tools (n8n, custom apps) to get value; level one (built-in AI features) and level two (Zapier or Make.com) automation meet the needs of individual knowledge workers

## Security & Safety Notes

- Avoid sharing sensitive personal data, passwords, API keys, or confidential business information inside AI chat interfaces unless the tool has explicit approval for that use case from your organization's security or IT team
- Review your company's data policies before uploading internal meeting transcripts, strategy documents, or customer data into third-party AI tools
- Be aware that meeting transcription tools such as Grain and Fathom handle potentially sensitive conversation data; understand their privacy and storage settings
- Do not treat AI-generated advice as gospel; always apply human judgment before acting on recommendations related to business, legal, medical, or financial decisions

## Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Skipping the five foundational habits in week one and jumping straight to advanced AI usage
  **Solution:** Pause and complete the five foundations first; everything else in the method builds on top of them

- **Problem:** Asking AI to do 100% of a task without human review, resulting in generic or low-quality output
  **Solution:** Use the 10/80/10 rule and always complete the first and final 10% yourself

- **Problem:** Using vague prompts without context and getting poor results
  **Solution:** Provide transcripts, examples, audience details, brand voice notes, and strategy documents as input before asking AI to generate outputs

- **Problem:** Repeating the same weak prompt without improving it over time
  **Solution:** Version your prompts like a recipe and refine them with each use based on the quality of the output

- **Problem:** Getting overwhelmed by the number of available AI tools
  **Solution:** Focus on tools that solve your specific work use cases instead of trying all new tools

- **Problem:** Automating tasks without first evaluating whether they should be automated, done manually, or removed entirely
  **Solution:** Before building any automation, decide if the process itself is worth keeping

- **Problem:** Blindly trusting AI output without applying taste or domain knowledge
  **Solution:** Filter all AI outputs through your own judgment, just as you would review a junior team member's work
